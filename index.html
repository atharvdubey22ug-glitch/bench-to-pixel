F²R · Optical computing

Bench to Pixel

In 2024 we built a half adder out of light on the physics lab's optical breadboard: a linearly polarised laser, three beamsplitters, two polarisers, a Michelson interferometer and photoresistors as detectors, with an Arduino applying the threshold. This page folds that bench onto a silicon chip, then zooms out until the chip is drawing a frame on a monitor.

On the bench · carry

An AND gate that adds intensities

Beamsplitter S1 (5) splits off the beam for input A and S2 (6) the beam for input B, and blocking a beam sets that input to 0. Our laser is linearly polarised, so polarisers (3, 4) turned to different angles give the two beams different powers, and mirrors (1, 2) bring both onto one photoresistor. With this intensity marking every combination of beams sums to a distinct reading, and only A and B together cross the Arduino's threshold.

On the bench · sum

An XOR gate from a dark fringe

The light that passes S2 reaches S3 (7), which splits it between mirrors (9) and (10) and recombines the returns into an interference pattern. Two lenses close together (8) enlarge it on a screen (11), and we cut a hole where a dark fringe falls. Blocking an arm sets that input to 0. With both arms open the fringes form and the hole stays dark. With one arm blocked the fringes vanish and light reaches the photoresistor behind the hole, the paper's "light from no light".

Sum apertureno light → 0

The two gates make a half adder, which gave the right output for all four input pairs. The full adder added a carry-in beam and used six beamsplitters, two interferometers and two AND gates feeding one photoresistor. That photoresistor could not reliably tell dark fringes from bright ones, and misalignment in the bulky setup lost light.

≈1 m → 0.4 mm

The same circuit in silicon

The bench flattens into a photonic circuit with the same layout. The paper's future-scope section pointed here: smaller setups with precise optics, then chips made with lithography. Every part has a standard counterpart in a silicon photonics process, and the light stays inside waveguides, which removes the three problems the full adder hit: weak detectors, misalignment and bulk.

Noise is no longer the problem the photoresistor was: the carry decision sits at Q = 56 and the sum at Q = 9, where 7 already means one error in 10¹². The XOR uses the most light, because its output passes each ring twice and leaves through a quarter-power port, so below 0.78 mW its error rate rises past one in 10¹².

The real limit is phase. The dark output holds only while the arms stay within ±19° of their bias, about 2.2 K of temperature difference between 100 µm arms, so a heater with feedback has to hold it. The paper found the same sensitivity in its fringe pattern on the bench.

Assumed values, all typical for silicon photonics at 1550 nm: ring radius 5 µm with 40 dB/cm doped loss and 40 pm/V drive, MMI excess loss 0.2 dB, loop mirrors 0.3 dB, photodiode 0.9 A/W, amplifier noise 20 pA/√Hz, laser noise −140 dB/Hz. The code is in the repo's sim/optical_sim.py.

0.4 mm → 3 mm

The carry gate is a dot product

The paper already treats AND as multiplication and sums polariser-weighted beams on one photoresistor. Read that as arithmetic: the carry gate splits a beam, weights each copy and sums the copies on one detector, which is y = wA·A + wB·B computed in a single pass of light. Give it four modulated inputs and four detectors and it multiplies a 4-vector by a 4×4 matrix: sixteen multiply-adds at once.

yi = Σj wij · xj   (i, j = 0…3)

GPUs run these sums constantly. Every vertex is multiplied by a 4×4 matrix, and every lit pixel takes a dot product between its surface normal and the light direction. Each neighbouring tile is that 4×4 multiplier: ring modulators on the left, amber weights at the crossings, photodiodes along the bottom. Sixty-four tiles make one block.

We simulated one tile with four wavelength channels 200 GHz apart, ring inputs driven by 6-bit DACs, 8-bit heater weights and an 8-bit ADC. At 0.5 mW per channel and 5 GS/s the result carries 5.6 effective bits, and noise sets that limit. More light helps until laser intensity noise and the input DACs cap it near 7 bits.

Vertex positions need 32-bit floats, far beyond 7 bits, so they would be split into bit-slices over several passes or stay on the electronic ALUs. Lighting tolerates a few bits; the monitor chapter shows how many.

3 mm → 7 mm

Inside a unified shader

The GPU around the tiles follows ATTILA, an open-source model of a Direct3D 9–era unified-shader GPU from the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya (ISPASS 2006, source released under a BSD-style licence). Its shader units run instructions such as DP3, DP4 and MAD, the dot products and multiply-adds the tiles are built for.

  • Fetch, decode and scheduling stay electronic.
  • The register file sends operands to drivers and DACs that set the ring modulators.
  • The 64-tile block does the dot products in light.
  • Photodiode currents return through TIAs and ADCs to the register file.
  • Texture sampling stays electronic.

In ATTILA's R600-style configuration each shader unit is a 16-lane SIMD array of four-wide vector ALUs plus a scalar ALU. A four-wide ALU does one DP4 per cycle; one optical tile does four per pass.

Built · standard · proposed

What on this page is real

Built

Optical AND, OR and XOR gates, a half adder that was correct for every input, and a full adder limited by photoresistor sensitivity, misalignment and bulk. Published in the Student Research Journal 2024.

Simulated

Circuit-level models of the half-adder cell and a 4×4 tile, with stated foundry-typical assumptions. The code and results are in the repo's sim/ folder.

Standard

MMI splitters, micro-ring modulators, variable attenuators, loop mirrors and germanium photodiodes are offered by silicon photonics foundries.

Proposed

The tile's place inside an ATTILA-style shader cluster. Nothing has been fabricated or simulated at the electromagnetic level, and every simulated number depends on the assumed parameters.

Open problems

Every pass converts values into light and back, and those DACs and ADCs cost energy and area. Precision is 5 to 7 bits per pass in our model. A ring modulator is about 10 µm across, hundreds of times wider than a transistor, so tiles are far less dense than logic. Laser power and waveguide loss limit how many tiles one source can feed.

Light helps by doing many multiply-adds in one pass. Electronics still schedules, stores and converts.
