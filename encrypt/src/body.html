What this shows

Both chips produce the same ciphertext at the same bit rate. The difference is how long a bit waits. Light reaches the photodiode about 19 ps after the modulators flip, and only 5.2 ps of that is the gate itself. The electronic chip must collect a whole word before its gates can act, so even a minimal design holds each bit for about three clock cycles, which is about 100 times longer. One coupler also replaces a whole array of gates.

What it doesn't show

  • Throughput is the same: both keep up with the line rate.
  • The electronic chip regenerates the signal with a fresh clock; the optical chip passes the inputs' timing jitter straight through.
  • The interference XOR needs message and key on one laser, so it fits a transmitter, not two unrelated fibres.
  • Energy is not compared. Both sides spend most of it in drivers and receivers at their edges.
  • Both latencies leave out the analog front ends (modulator drivers and photodiode amplifier; receiver clock recovery and equalisation), so the electronic figure is a floor.

How each side is computed

Optical: your bits drive the time-domain model of the single-pass XOR layout (FastXOR, SiEPIC extraction), in a worker in this page. It is the same model as the Python study, matching it to 1e-16. Modulators and photodiode have Gaussian responses at the chosen bandwidth; 10 mW laser, 1 A/W photodiode, 20 pA/√Hz amplifier noise. Each bit is decided at its centre, 19 ps after it was sent. The errors you see come out of that simulation.

Electronic: a model, not a simulation. Word width W = ⌈rate ÷ clock⌉; a bit waits one word to be collected, one clock for the XOR register and one to cross into the transmit clock: three word times.
