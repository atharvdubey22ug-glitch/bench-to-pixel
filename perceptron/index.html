A neuron made of light

Sixteen arms, one pass. Each input pixel and its weight set one arm's phase to 0 or π. The light of all sixteen arms adds up, and two photodiodes read the sum with its sign. Store a digit as the weights and the sum says how closely the input matches it: 16 minus twice the number of pixels that differ. Draw a digit below. The chip compares it with all ten stored digits, one pass of light each, and every number on this page comes from the simulated light of the drawn chip.

How exact is the sum?

The chip is calibrated after fabrication using only its two output readings, in three steps: each arm's phase; a map of how much each heater warms its neighbours, which the driver then cancels pattern by pattern; and a weight on one photodiode that keeps the output a straight line when the output coupler is not exactly 50/50. Below, all 65,536 settings of the sixteen arms on this simulated die: the spread of the output at each value of the sum, against the gap to the next value.

How fast: one image per bit slot

Time-domain simulation of the drawn circuit: a stream of digit images (random digits, up to two pixels flipped) drives the sixteen arms, one whole image per bit slot. The weights hold the stored 7 and the neuron fires when the sum is at least 12. The sections sit at different points along their arms, about 15 ps apart, so each input is delayed by its section's position (aligned). They are treated as fast phase modulators placed where the heaters are.

The chip

Drawn in KLayout with the SiEPIC EBeam process design kit (Applied Nanotools): 220 nm silicon waveguides, titanium-tungsten heaters, a metal routing layer. The two trees are mirror images and every arm is a straight line between them, so all sixteen paths are the same length; the reference arm is routed to match them.

What is real here

The layout passes the foundry's checks. The light comes from a circuit simulation of the extracted layout with the design kit's component models, at nine process corners, with heater spread, random arm offsets and thermal crosstalk on every simulated die, calibrated from the two output readings alone. The page computes the outputs from a sum over the seventeen waveguides fitted to that simulation, which leaves out reflections between arms and sits within 0.1 to 0.27 of a level of it, depending on the corner; the speed runs use the same circuit in the time domain. Nothing is fabricated yet.

Where it comes from

On the bench, polarisers marked beams with weights and a photoresistor added their intensities: that carry gate was already a weighted sum. The parity chip showed that phase, 0 or π, carries a bit through interference without loss of meaning. Put the phases side by side instead of in a row and the light adds them: a binary-weight neuron, the XNOR-and-count unit of binarised neural networks, and a matched filter for the stored pattern.

What it does not do

  • Weights are only +1 or −1. Values in between need an amplitude control (one more interferometer) per arm.
  • One neuron: ten digits take ten passes here, or ten copies of the chip side by side.
  • The decision itself, and anything after it, is electronic. A deep network would need light-to-light nonlinearity or a detector between layers.
  • Each arm gets one sixteenth of the light; wider neurons need more laser power.
